The contemporary landscape of increasing infrastructure projects largely funded and managed by private companies has become prevalent across many countries. Although this development may significantly reduce the burden on the government's burden, I believe it is negative, as it neglects domestic social demands and accountability.

On the one hand, many free-market economists emphasize the financial benefits of private investment and management. Several advocates claim governments are obliged to allocate public expenditure across disparate sectors, yet the taxpayers' money is insufficient for capital-intensive infrastructure projects. This financial shortfall can be compensated for to some extent through private-sector investment. In Vietnam and other developing countries, for instance, a number of municipal planning schemes are greatly funded and well-maintained by large corporate consortia like Vin Group. Not only does this solid financial foundation improve the overall quality of urban living, but it also fosters economic prosperity, as many parties, ranging from contractors to construction suppliers, are able to secure profitable public projects.

Despite the aforementioned benefits, I believe privatization is mainly negative due to the fact that such privately funded initiatives tend to cater primarily to the upper-income group rather than the broader public.  For example, within the metropolitan areas of Ha Noi, a multitude of housing schemes are privately funded and sold at exorbitant prices, whereas government-backed social housing programs are extremely scarce and subject to intense competition, making it harder for lower-income households to afford a settlement. Moreover, private businesses driven by profits rely on substandards material and overlook safety standards, including fire prevention systems and emergency exits. In fact, many private apartment complexes have deteriorated over the past few years in many countries, ultimately leading to additional costs for resident relocation and renovations. In these respects, private investment and management are regarded as disadvantageous when social implications are not taken into consideration.

In conclusion, while it is beneficial for governments to receive financial support from non-state corporations, I firmly contend that this trend engenders more drawbacks in the years ahead. Large projects should be partially invested in by corporate businesses and simultaneously operated under governmental surveillance to ensure better accountability, public accessibility, and long-term sustainability.
