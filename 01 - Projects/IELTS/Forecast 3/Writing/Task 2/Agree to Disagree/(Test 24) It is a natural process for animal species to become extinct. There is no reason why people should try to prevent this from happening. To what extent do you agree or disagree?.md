Some people argue that because extinction is a natural process, humans have no reason to prevent it. Personally, I strongly disagree with this stance. Although some animal species disappear without human interference, it is the preventable losses caused by our own activities that we cannot afford to ignore, along with the wider anthropogenic consequences for ecosystems.

The primary justification for human intervention is the fundamental asymmetry between natural extinction and anthropogenic species loss. Human activities, namely deforestation, aquatic pollution, and overhunting, imperil wildlife populations that would otherwise thrive. Labeling this modern crisis as a natural evolutionary phase conveniently obscures human agency in driving these environmental pressures. Creatures reliant on that habitat for sustenance and shelter, for instance, are abruptly displaced or left to starve. Therefore, prioritizing habitat preservation over arbitrary environmental manipulation represents the most effective strategy to mitigate human-induced ecological damage.

Another compelling argument to consider is that species are intricately woven into interconnected ecological systems, meaning that the loss of a single organism can trigger a domino effect across many others. Should animal species disappear, disruptions to food webs, predation dynamics, and resource competition will ensue, directly imperiling wider fauna. Furthermore, species facilitate indispensable ecological functions, such as pollination and seed dispersal, which are vital for both natural habitats and human livelihoods. Allowing animals to vanish while disregarding their ecological interdependence could catalyze catastrophic crises that are irreversible and prohibitively expensive to rectify. 

Admittedly, conservation cannot guarantee the survival of every single species, which means public funding must be allocated strategically. However, rather than abandoning environmental protection, policymakers should view these limitations as grounds for establishing clear-cut priorities. Ultimately, preserving environmental habitats and minimizing human activities that threaten other animals can offer wider ecological and socio-economic benefits than focusing exclusively on individual animals.

In conclusion, the fact that extinction occurs naturally does not mean it should be accepted widely by society. Seldom should we overlook our responsibility to prevent avoidable extinction, particularly when anthropogenic activities inflict such severe damage and jeopardize the stability of the entire biosphere.
