Some people argue that because extinction is a natural process, humans have no reason to prevent it. Personally, I strongly disagree with this stance. Although some animal species disappear without human interference, it is the preventable losses caused by our own activities that we cannot afford to ignore, along with the wider anthropogenic consequences for ecosystems.

The first reason we should intervene is that natural extinction and human-driven extinction are not equivalent. Activities such as clearing off forests, polluting waterways, and hunting animals excessively threaten animal species that might otherwise survive. By describing their disappearance as natural, people tend to overlook their active role in creating these environmental pressures. To illustrate this, when a forest is cleared for economic development, animals depending on this ecosystem for shelter and food suffer heavily. Therefore, it is the protection of their remaining habitats, rather than unnecessary interference with nature, that effectively reduces the damage caused by humans.

Another reason to consider is species form a part of interconnected ecological systems, meaning that the loss of one animal can easily affect many others. Should animal species disappear, changes to food chains, predation, and competition will ensue, which directly jeopardize surrounding wildlife. Furthermore, creatures contribute to vital processes, such as pollination and seed dispersal supporting both natural vegetation and human livelihoods. Not only does conservation protect individual creatures, but it also delivers practical value to the entire ecosystem. Allowing species to die out without considering their affinity could create severe problems that are difficult or expensive to reverse.
